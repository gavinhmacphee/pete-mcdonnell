Potential Is Not Always Visible.

Reframing late developers. Why selection meetings reward the obvious — and what the best academies do instead.

Pete McDonnell / May 2026 / 6 min read

Every selection meeting I have sat in has the same gravitational pull. Coaches default to what they can see right now — speed, athleticism, the player who controls the room because the ball goes through them. The meeting is fast, the bias toward the obvious is strong, and the player who is six months from his growth spurt does not jump off the page.

I have made this mistake. I have sat in rooms where we picked a 16-year-old over a 14-year-old and called it talent identification, when what we were really doing was rewarding a body that had finished a job earlier. Two years later, the 14-year-old was the better player. The 16-year-old was still the same.

Talent identification is the part of the job we get wrong most often, because we are confusing performance with potential, and we are doing it under time pressure.

The data that already exists

There is now a deep body of research — relative age effects, late-developer studies, the bio-banding work coming out of England — that confirms what every honest coach already suspects: the player who dominates at 13 is rarely the one who signs a contract at 18. Birthdays cluster in the early part of the year. Early-maturers get the academy spots. Late developers get released.

This is not new. What is new is that some of the best academies in the world are now treating it as a system problem instead of a personnel problem.

At Borussia Dortmund, players are routinely played up an age group not as a reward but as a tool — to expose them to overload, to make the game harder, to force adaptation. At Ajax, identification is a multi-year process with explicit do-not-release windows when the body is doing something the eye cannot see. The principle in both cases is the same.

You cannot evaluate a player whose puberty is mid-sentence.

What this changes for clubs

Three things, and all three sit at the level of process, not personality.

One — you stop releasing on a single bad cycle. Most clubs make selection decisions in March, after a winter where late developers got physically dominated for three months in a row. That is the worst possible window. Build a calendar that forces a second look in June, then again in September, after the player has had a chance to grow.

Two — you separate readiness from ability. A 14-year-old who is small and undeveloped is not a worse player than a 14-year-old who is large and dominant. He is the same player at a different point. Your selection paperwork should make this distinction explicit. If it does not, you will keep losing the player whose body was late.

Three — you ask better questions in the room. Not "is he good?" but "what is this player when his body catches up?" Not "did he stand out today?" but "what is the trend across nine sessions?" Selection meetings should not ask questions the eye can answer. They should ask questions the eye cannot.

The player who proves the point

I think about a player from earlier in my career. He would not have made the roster by any traditional measure. Small, slow, no obvious dominant trait. Already released by one program at 13.

What he had was a brain. Decision-making sharper than any of his peers. We held the spot. Inside fourteen months he grew six inches. The brain was still there. Now the body was too. He went on to be one of the best players in his age group.

That player is not a story about talent. That player is a story about a system that resisted the urge to release him on the obvious read.

The framing that needs to die

"Late developer" is the wrong phrase. It implies the player is behind. He is not. He is on his own timeline, and the only thing late about him is your evaluation of him.

Potential is not always visible. The job is not to find the player who is already there. The job is to build an environment that holds the door open long enough for the player who is becoming one.
